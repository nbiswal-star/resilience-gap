Context

Severe storms, floods, hurricanes and winter storms damage U.S. communities every year. In NOAA's Storm Events Database, events that can be placed in a U.S. county from 2000 to 2025 record at least $818 billion in property and crop damage in 2025 dollars ($575 billion nominal; our own tally from the bulk files, adjusted with the BLS Consumer Price Index). Two federal channels respond, and in both a county usually reaches FEMA through a state-level process. After an incident, a governor or tribal chief executive requests a presidential declaration; a major disaster declaration designates specific counties (rarely a whole state) for FEMA assistance, or the request is turned down. Before the next incident, FEMA's Hazard Mitigation Assistance (HMA) programs fund projects such as buyouts, drainage and safe rooms. States, territories and tribes act as applicants: they solicit, screen and prioritize local subapplications and forward selected ones to FEMA for review; funds are obligated only later. Communities also differ in their capacity to prepare and recover, which the CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) measures for most U.S. counties and all 78 Puerto Rico municipios.

Problem statement

Central question: how do presidential disaster declarations and recorded HMA obligations align with county storm losses and social vulnerability? We will answer it with an end-to-end pipeline that integrates 2000–2025 NOAA and FEMA records with 2022 CDC/ATSDR vulnerability scores and July 2022 Census population estimates at the county level, and show where recorded federal response and recorded losses diverge most. The analysis is descriptive, not causal, and keeps four measures separate because each is observed at a different stage:

  1. Declaration coverage: a significant event-county pair falls inside a weather-related major disaster declaration that designates its county (or literally the whole state), with a compatible hazard whose event date falls within the declaration incident period, allowing the implemented one-day boundary tolerance.
  2. Observed HMA subapplication activity: the county is named as a benefiting area in a subapplication published in OpenFEMA, i.e. one that reached FEMA. Proposals that stopped at the local or state stage are invisible.
  3. Approval / status: the subapplication's current recorded status (e.g. "Submitted to FEMA", "Approved", "Obligated"), a snapshot rather than a final outcome.
  4. Obligation: FEMA's recorded obligated amount in 2025 dollars, split equally across every benefiting-area entry a subapplication lists. Shares for statewide, out-of-state, unresolved or missing areas stay at state level rather than being guessed; these are about 18 % of obligated dollars.

Specific questions

  1. Exposure. How is recorded storm damage per resident distributed across SVI quartiles, and which counties carry the heaviest recorded burden?
  2. Declaration coverage. What share of significant event-county pairs (≥ $100,000 of county damage in 2025 dollars, or any casualty) has declaration coverage, and how does it vary by SVI quartile, hazard family, FEMA region and state? As government-level context only: how many weather-related declaration requests were turned down? (DeclarationDenials records carry no county and are not HMA decisions.)
  3. Mitigation alignment, stage by stage. By five-year period, program and hazard family, is a county's HMA activity (a) not observed, (b) observed without a positive obligation, or (c) obligated, and how do these stages, the status mix, total obligation and weather-related obligation per resident vary with recorded losses and SVI? Follow-up screen: top-quartile damage per resident, SVI Q4 and total obligation per resident ≤ the HMA-comparable states + DC median. It is not evidence that a county was overlooked or never applied.
  4. Timeliness. How many days pass from incident start to the request and to the declaration, and how do these lags differ by SVI group, hazard family and FEMA region?

Robustness: equal vs. population-weighted allocation of county-attributed HMA shares; alternative significance thresholds. Stretch: National Flood Insurance Program claims vs. recorded flood damage.

What the data can and cannot show

An observed zero obligation is ambiguous: no subapplication naming the county reached FEMA; the state screened out or deprioritized one; local capacity (staff, cost share, benefit-cost analysis) limited what was prepared; a subapplication is pending, withdrawn or unsuccessful; the county is covered only by a statewide or unparsed entry; or it is a true zero. The data cannot separate these, so we report the stage at which activity is or is not observed and never infer that a county did not apply, or why its funding is low. Declarations follow state requests and FEMA criteria, not NOAA records, so low coverage is a divergence to explain, not neglect. NOAA damage figures are estimates, and SVI comparisons are unadjusted associations, not effects of vulnerability.

Why it matters, and to whom

  • State hazard mitigation officers and county emergency managers can see where recorded losses and vulnerability are high but recorded obligations low: a starting point for outreach and technical assistance within the state's own HMA process.
  • FEMA regional staff, legislators and auditors get a transparent, stage-by-stage county measure of how declarations and mitigation obligations track recorded need, which bears on ongoing debates about equity in disaster programs.
  • Community organizations in highly vulnerable counties get reproducible evidence, with its limits stated, to support questions to state and federal agencies.
  • Researchers and insurers get a cleaned, integrated county panel that links hazards, federal response and vulnerability, which no single public source provides.

All sources are public and need no API key. We have already confirmed access and ingested every source; a working pipeline builds the Q1–Q4 serving tables, and the per-county HMA stage and status breakdown is the main extension for Milestones 2–3. Structured data goes into PostgreSQL in separate raw, staging, curated and serving layers; original files and JSON responses are kept unchanged in object storage with an S3-compatible path. This product uses the Federal Emergency Management Agency's OpenFEMA API, but is not endorsed by FEMA.
